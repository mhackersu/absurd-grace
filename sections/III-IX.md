# Chapter 9: The Algorithm Will Not Save You: Or, How We Baptized Narcissus and Called It Discipleship

In the beginning was the Word—and now the Word has been trimmed to 280 characters, run through Canva, hashtagged, and posted with a ring light. Welcome to the age of platformed piety, where the fruit of the Spirit is measured in engagement metrics, and sanctification is synonymous with personal branding.

We have not just wandered into Babylon; we’ve scheduled content for it.

## Worshipping at the Altar of Image

In this brave new world, our gods are verified and our prophets are monetized. The imago Dei has been cropped, filtered, and photoshopped into a lifestyle aesthetic. As Kierkegaard warned in *The Present Age*, the age of reflection without action produces people who mistake commentary for commitment. We’ve become spiritual voyeurs, scrolling past the suffering of the world with a sad emoji and a vague promise to “pray.”

And the algorithm rewards us for it.

Jesus said, “When you pray, go into your room and shut the door.” Today we livestream our quiet times, tag our Bibles, and post our tears with moody presets. The paradox is stunning: the more we brand ourselves as authentic, the less we seem to remember how to actually be human.

## From Desert Fathers to Digital Fathers

Remember the early monastics who fled to the desert to escape the corrupting influence of empire? Now we’ve got digital ascetics who “detox” by switching from Instagram to Substack. We’ve traded caves for curated content.

In *Disruptive Witness*, Alan Noble warns of the way modern distractions deform our spiritual attentiveness. He’s not wrong. But the problem runs deeper. We’re not just distracted—we’re discipled by platforms that demand constant production. To be seen is to exist. To disappear is to die.

This is not simply a shift in medium—it is a shift in meaning.

## Spurgeon Would Never Have Had a TikTok

Charles Spurgeon, who preached to thousands without a microphone, once said, “A time will come when instead of shepherds feeding the sheep, the church will have clowns entertaining the goats.” That time has come, and the clowns have agents.

Influence, in our day, is often mistaken for anointing. As a result, pastors chase aesthetics instead of holiness. Theology is flattened into bite-sized inspiration. Sermons are edited for “shareability.” Christ is clickbait.

And Kierkegaard sighs from the grave: “There comes a midnight hour when everyone has to throw off his mask.” Yet we’ve built whole ministries on the mask. Worse, we call the mask ministry.

## The Hollow Echo of Self

What we have now is not discipleship—it’s a hall of mirrors, a brand-friendly gospel that echoes our preferences back at us. This is what Eugene Peterson feared when he critiqued “the pastor as program director.” The shepherd has been replaced by a content strategist, the flock by a follower count.

Wendell Berry once wrote that community cannot be downloaded, that love is too particular for general platforms. But we keep trying. We market Jesus like an energy drink: quick results, flashy packaging, zero calories of conviction.

## The Gospel of Upgrades

What’s truly diabolical is how normal it feels. The algorithm rewards our hustle. It whispers: Post more. Prove more. Preach harder. Perform better. And we do it, not because we’re wicked, but because we’re exhausted. Because obscurity feels like failure in an age where everyone’s profile is a résumé.

We treat grace like a software update—something to improve the self rather than crucify it. But as Bonhoeffer wrote, “When Christ calls a man, he bids him come and die.” Death doesn’t trend well. So we dress it up. Rebrand the cross. Market the tomb. Spin resurrection as a lifestyle enhancement.

It’s all absurd. Absurd grace—uncool, unmarketable, and inconveniently true—interrupts this parade of posturing.

## The Return to Sacred Obscurity

What if we logged off? Not as a performative fast, but as an act of resistance? What if we rediscovered the strange holiness of being unknown?

Kierkegaard’s “witness to the truth” doesn’t chase applause. It stands alone in the public square and speaks, knowing full well the crowd prefers entertainment. And maybe, just maybe, grace shows up not in virality but in obscurity. In small acts of love no one will ever see. In faithfulness without applause.

## Conclusion: From Followers to Faith

We don’t need more influencers. We need more saints. People who will trade platforms for prayer closets, who will choose faithfulness over fame, who understand that the algorithm will not save us.

The Body of Christ is not a brand. The Church is not a content factory. And grace is not a growth hack.

It’s time to shut down the app, light a candle, and remember the absurdity of it all: that while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us—not to go viral, but to go deep.

And if that makes us uncool, so be it.

Let the fools confound the wise.
Let grace crash the marketplace.
Let us live unmarketable lives.

Amen.
