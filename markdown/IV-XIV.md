# Chapter 14: **What If the Internet Isn’t Our Mission Field?**  
*Subtitle: Reframing ministry in a hyperconnected age.*

In a world where Wi-Fi is ubiquitous and the number of people with internet access far outpaces the number of those without, it’s easy to think of the internet as the mission field of the 21st century. After all, if the Great Commission calls us to go into all the world and preach the gospel, the digital world seems like an obvious place to go. But what if the internet, with its endless scroll and distraction, isn’t the mission field we think it is? What if our ministry efforts online need to be reconsidered—not as a replacement for true incarnational ministry, but as a tool within a larger, more holistic vision of what it means to spread the message of Jesus?



## **The Digital Revolution and the Overlooked Dangers of Hyperconnectivity**

There is no denying the monumental shift the internet has caused in the way we connect, communicate, and experience life. The rise of social media, the proliferation of online platforms, and the almost constant availability of information have redefined human relationships and societal values. However, while this hyperconnectivity seems to offer limitless potential for outreach and engagement, it also comes with unintended consequences—many of which undermine genuine human connection and spiritual depth.

The internet, in all its vastness, can be a hollow substitute for true, embodied ministry. As the digital landscape grows, so too does a subtle, yet growing sense of alienation. Research continually shows how the more people interact with digital media, the less satisfied they feel with their real-world relationships. Despite being more "connected" than ever before, we are also more isolated, more prone to anxiety, and more disconnected from the people physically around us. It seems we’re living in a paradox where the more we engage online, the less we engage with the life that happens outside the screen.

Ministry, in its truest sense, cannot be fully replicated in the realm of digital platforms. The beauty of the gospel—the grace of presence, the power of touch, and the depth of face-to-face conversations—is lost in an environment where everything is reduced to pixels and text. 



## **The Fallacy of "Preaching to the Digital Choir"**

Another danger in viewing the internet as the mission field is the temptation to preach primarily to the choir. While online ministry can reach millions of people, it also tends to amplify messages within already like-minded communities. It’s easy to forget that the internet is not a neutral space. Algorithms tailor what we see, hear, and consume, often reinforcing pre-existing beliefs and preferences. A sermon posted online, while available to the masses, is most likely to be consumed by those already inclined to agree with it.

The danger is that ministry becomes little more than an echo chamber, where pastors and ministries preach to the already-converted, ensuring that their followers continue to follow, but not necessarily making new disciples. This model of ministry, where we engage only with people who think like us or share our views, is not the model of gospel outreach Jesus demonstrated.

Jesus didn’t just sit down and wait for crowds to form around him. He went to the broken, the marginalized, the outcasts. He spent time with people who were different, who were often ignored by the religious elite. When we only engage with people who already know the gospel, we fail to follow the example of Jesus who reached out to those who had never heard the message at all.



## **The Misplaced Focus on Online Metrics: More Followers ≠ More Disciples**

In the age of digital ministry, numbers matter. Ministries are often evaluated based on views, shares, likes, and followers. We judge success by the metrics that are easily accessible and measurable in the digital space. But these metrics, while valuable for gauging reach, don’t necessarily tell the full story.

True discipleship cannot be reduced to a numerical value. The depth of transformation that the gospel brings to an individual’s life cannot be measured in likes or retweets. The challenge of digital ministry is to resist the temptation to chase numbers and instead focus on creating genuine, lasting relationships with people.

In the digital age, we can become obsessed with increasing our reach, thinking that the more people we speak to, the more impact we’re having. But the gospel isn’t about mass consumption—it’s about personal transformation. Jesus didn’t send out the twelve disciples to preach to millions. He invested in a small group, poured his life into them, and sent them out to transform the world. Numbers are not irrelevant, but they are secondary to the quality of relationships and the depth of transformation.



## **The Irreplaceable Power of Incarnational Ministry**

The heart of the Christian mission has always been incarnational. The Word became flesh and dwelt among us (John 1:14). Jesus didn’t stay distant or aloof. He didn’t send us instructions from afar—he came down into our world, lived among us, and demonstrated the truth of God in human form. He showed us that ministry is about being with people, not just sending information to them.

Online ministry, no matter how impactful or widespread, cannot replace the need for real, physical presence. Discipleship involves walking with people through life—the highs, the lows, the messy parts, and the beautiful moments. It means sitting down over coffee, holding someone's hand during difficult times, and walking through the ups and downs of life together. These moments of human connection, grounded in love, compassion, and presence, are what make the gospel come alive.



## **Redefining the Mission Field: Local and Global, Digital and Personal**

So, does this mean we should abandon the internet as a mission field? Not necessarily. The internet, like any tool, can be used for good or ill. It’s a space where ideas can be shared, where communities can be formed, and where people can hear the message of Christ who might never step foot in a church building. The digital space offers incredible potential for outreach, education, and discipleship.

But we must be mindful that the internet is just that—a tool, not the mission itself. Ministry should never be confined to a screen. The Great Commission calls us to make disciples of all nations, but it also calls us to live out the gospel in real, tangible ways. Digital ministry can be a starting point, but it cannot replace the real-world, embodied ministry that Jesus modeled.

Instead of seeing the internet as the mission field, we should view it as a resource that can complement, not replace, the mission field. Our primary focus must still be on the local and global communities around us—the people we interact with daily, the neighbors we pass in the street, the family members we spend holidays with. The internet can be a bridge, but it should not become the destination.



## **Conclusion: A Call to Discipleship, Not Digital Domination**

As we navigate ministry in a hyperconnected world, we must remember that the true heart of the mission is not digital domination or online influence—it is discipleship. It’s about following Jesus’ example of presence, relationship, and personal transformation. It’s about going into the world and making disciples, not just amassing followers.

The internet is a tool, not the mission itself. It can be used for good, but it can also be a distraction from what really matters. We must use digital platforms wisely, recognizing their potential while never losing sight of the need for true, incarnational ministry. 

Let us not forget the call to go into all the world—but let us also remember that the world includes our local communities, our physical neighborhoods, and the real, embodied relationships that are the heart of the gospel. Our mission is not defined by the tools we use, but by the message we carry and the way we live it out.
