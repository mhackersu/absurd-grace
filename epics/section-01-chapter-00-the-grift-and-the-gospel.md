# Chapter 1: The Grift and the Gospel

> “If anyone would come after me, let him deny himself and take up his cross and follow me.”  
> — Jesus of Nazareth, Gospel of Mark 8:34

## Introduction

There is a line between sacred and profane, though in today’s culture, that line is blurred, smeared, memed, sold back to you as a hoodie, and marketed as an aesthetic. Welcome to the age of the grift.

Every generation inherits its own style of manipulation. Ours? Ours' packages transcendence in subscriptions. It offers identity in branding. It sells "soul" in a lifestyle bundle, $49.99 per month — cancel anytime. 

And the Gospel? It's either sold as product or sanitized for Instagram. But at its core, it is something too wild, too unmanageable to be merchandised. It is scandalous. Unreasonable. It is, in a word, absurd.

This chapter sets the stage for a journey through the tensions between sincerity and spectacle, depth and shallowness, God and mammon.

## The Mechanics of the Grift

The grift is not just an act of deception. It is a system. A method of leveraging belief, longing, and the human desire for purpose to engineer control. It works best when it looks like liberation. And in our time, it wears the robes of progress, empowerment, or even piety.

**Examples of the Grift in Practice:**

- Mega-influencers using Christianese to build brand empires.
- Tech companies invoking spiritual language to sell digital escapism.
- Even churches adopting growth strategies from venture capital playbooks.

### Kierkegaard's Warning

Søren Kierkegaard saw it coming. He wrote that Christendom — the institutionalization of Christianity — would become its own betrayal. A system that protects comfort and power while naming Jesus just enough to maintain legitimacy. He warned that true Christianity was always personal, always risky. That it demands a leap of faith, not a branding deal.

The grift makes a copy of the sacred. But like a counterfeit bill, it lacks the watermark of suffering. It cannot bleed. It cannot resurrect.

## The Gospel That Cannot Be Bought

Enter the Gospel. Not the self-help version. Not the American Dream remix. But the original: the message that the infinite Creator loves finite, broken humanity so much that He entered the story — lived, died, and rose again — not to sell a product, but to reconcile us.

This is not profitable.

Not scalable.

Not algorithmically optimized.

It doesn’t promise success. It doesn’t guarantee your breakthrough. It simply calls you — absurdly — to die and live again.

### Charles Spurgeon on the Offense of the Gospel

Spurgeon preached that the Gospel offends human pride. It undermines every attempt to earn righteousness, to perform for divine approval. That’s why the grift must neuter it — turn it into law, not grace. Into performance, not mercy. Into transaction, not transformation.

> "The Gospel is like a lion. You don’t need to defend it. Just let it loose." — Spurgeon

## God's Love: So Big It’s Absurd

Here’s the joke that no marketing team can quite monetize: God's love is too big to fit in the pitch deck. Too expansive to be constrained to the backend of a nonprofit’s quarterly report. Too scandalous to pass through committee.

His love is absurd in its inclusiveness — while remaining exclusive in its path.

Absurd in its offer: “Come and die.”

Absurd in its economy: The last shall be first.

Absurd in its climax: The King wears a crown of thorns.

To the watching world, this looks like failure. Weakness. A poor ROI.

But it is the power of God.

## Comedy as Theology: Laughing at the Grift

If you can’t laugh at the grift, you’ll cry. And crying’s okay, but let’s start with satire.

Imagine:

- A televangelist trying to resurrect Lazarus on TikTok Live.
- A sermon series titled: “Branding Like Jesus: Leveraging Parables for Market Growth.”
- Jesus flipping tables… now with influencer collabs and a Shopify link in bio.

Comedy unearths the ridiculous by exaggerating the real. It unveils what we’ve accepted as normal and quietly says: “Wait, what?”

It is the holy foolishness that mocks the proud and humbles the wise. A tool Kierkegaard himself used — often writing under pseudonyms to poke fun at the very institutions he critiqued.

Comedy, in this book, becomes not just entertainment but prophetic voice. Sacred satire. A mirror, and a hammer.

## Indigenous Memory and the Long View

Robin Wall Kimmerer, in *Braiding Sweetgrass*, speaks of knowing the world through relationship, reciprocity, and responsibility. Her writing, while not overtly Christian, pulses with spiritual resonance.

The Gospel, properly understood, is not just individual salvation. It is cosmic restoration. It is justice. Healing. Reconciliation between Creator and creation.

The grift separates us from the land, the past, and one another. The Gospel unites.

In the story of indigenous peoples, we find echoes of God’s heart for stewardship, humility, and sacred memory — values easily erased in a profit-first culture.

## Martyrdom in a Comfortable Age

Today, no lions wait in the Colosseum. But martyrdom comes in other forms:

- Social ostracization.
- Career derailment.
- Public shaming for standing in grace and truth.

In the age of grift, saying “Jesus is Lord” may still cost you — not your life, perhaps, but your comfort, your platform, your perceived relevance.

And yet, the witness remains.

Martyrdom (from *martus*, meaning "witness") is still the shape of faith. Not performative suffering, but a life poured out.

## A Gospel Without a Hook

The Gospel doesn't need a hook. It doesn't need an upsell. It doesn’t need to “convert” better.

It is good news.

Full stop.

We don't sell it. We live it. We carry it. We die for it if we must.

And in doing so, we expose the grift — not with rage, but with radiant joy. With absurd grace.

## Closing Thoughts

The first step in escaping the grift is recognizing the absurdity of grace. That God — the infinite, the holy, the just — would love *you*, would love *me*, with a love that cannot be earned or bought, is too much. It's offensive. It's comic. It's glorious.

That’s the axis on which this entire book turns.

The world will keep trying to sell you purpose. Package spirituality. Monetize virtue.

But grace? Grace still comes without cost — except to the One who paid it all.
