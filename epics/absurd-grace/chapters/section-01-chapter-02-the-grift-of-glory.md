# Chapter 3: The Grift of Glory

_**How empires monetize miracles, and why the gospel doesn’t sell well on merch.**_

Throughout history, there has been a fascinating intersection between faith and commerce. There’s a pattern that runs through centuries of empire-building, where figures of power—be they religious leaders, kings, or cultural icons—are often commodified for profit. In a world where faith is supposed to be about the sacred, something more transcendent than material wealth, we’ve seen the transformation of religion and spirituality into a profitable enterprise. This chapter will explore this dynamic through the lens of both historical exploitation narratives and the contemporary commercialization of religion.

The commercialization of religion has often been so subtle that we may not even realize when we’re witnessing it. We buy books, holy relics, souvenirs from temples, and, of course, the modern megachurch merchandise. If it weren’t for its deep historical roots, it would be hard to draw the line between something like a branded t-shirt from a church and a piece of religious iconography. But the relationship between faith and commerce has been with us for centuries, and it’s been shaped by social systems that understand one thing: people will pay for what they believe in.

### The First Grift: The History of Exploitation

One of the most powerful historical accounts of exploitation, particularly how people’s faith was manipulated for profit, comes from *Killers of the Flower Moon* by David Grann. The book tells the haunting tale of the Osage people, who were systematically exploited after discovering oil on their land in the early 20th century. The Osage were targeted by unscrupulous individuals who had no problem monetizing the inherent wealth and rights of the native people. Yet, this wasn't just about oil. This was a more insidious kind of grift. It was about power, control, and the commodification of life itself. The exploitation wasn't simply financial—it was personal, emotional, and even spiritual in its deep impact on the Osage people.

The Osage murders are a dark reminder of how institutions of power (whether political, corporate, or religious) can manipulate vulnerable populations for personal gain. And the Osage weren’t the only ones who suffered under this system. This pattern stretches across history, where the 'mystical' or 'miraculous' is used to draw people in, promising divine salvation or personal enlightenment, only to turn into a form of economic exploitation.

For the Osage, their wealth was viewed as something to be taken, and in the case of religion, something to be packaged and sold. The commodification of spiritual or emotional resources has always been part of a greater scheme—a scheme designed not to elevate, but to exploit. Whether it’s a preacher profiting off of a ‘faith healer’ persona or a politician using religion as a tool for social control, the idea remains the same: convert the sacred into the profitable. 

### Spurgeon’s Grace and the Commercialization of the Divine

Enter Charles Spurgeon, the renowned 19th-century preacher whose passionate sermons and fervent belief in the grace of God brought comfort to millions. Spurgeon’s theology emphasized God’s unconditional love for humanity—a grace that was available to all, no matter their wealth or status. Yet, even Spurgeon’s theology is subject to commodification. His writings, his image, and his teachings were disseminated far and wide, but the way the public consumed those teachings inevitably revealed a conflict: the gospel, as Spurgeon preached it, didn’t quite align with the ‘church as brand’ model.

In modern Christianity, we see this all too often. Churches sell merchandise, Christian bookshops peddle self-help books alongside Bibles, and televangelists preach for donations, often positioning salvation as something to be obtained through financial giving. The gospel, a message of grace, has been twisted into a product that can be sold. It’s not enough to preach God’s word—one must also sell it, brand it, and make it appeal to the masses, often through the promise of prosperity and success.

The ‘prosperity gospel’ is a classic example of this commercialization. It commodifies the idea of spiritual health and financial well-being, framing them as direct correlations. If you’re faithful enough, you’ll be rewarded with riches. This is a grift, plain and simple. It distorts the true message of self-sacrificial love and gives followers false promises of material gain.

But is this really a new phenomenon? The answer is no. From the indulgences of the Catholic Church in the Middle Ages to today’s megachurches, the grift of glory has been a part of religious history. The more desperate the situation, the easier it is to prey on people’s hopes, especially in a world where religion has often been a crutch for the marginalized and powerless.

### Faith, Glory, and the Modern Empire

In today’s world, we see this same grift playing out in more subtle ways. Influencers, megachurch leaders, and celebrities have figured out how to monetize faith, spirituality, and self-help in ways that are shockingly efficient. We live in a time where personal branding is everything, and religion, spirituality, and personal growth are just another category to be commodified.

The Empire of Glory is no longer a metaphorical place. It is everywhere, wrapped in the glowing sheen of social media and the growing power of corporate-backed religion. The same people who sell us diet pills and miracle cures are often the same ones offering ‘life-changing’ spiritual guidance for a price. In fact, much of the Christian community has become hyper-commercialized. Churches themselves are now corporations, competing for revenue and influence as much as they do for souls.

But the grift of glory is not limited to any single religion or faith. It spans across cultures and beliefs, exploiting the human desire for transcendence. We buy ‘lucky charms,’ attend ‘motivational seminars,’ and subscribe to spiritual gurus, all in the pursuit of a better life. We are constantly told that the key to greatness—whether that’s financial, personal, or spiritual—is just one purchase away.

The danger here is not just that these products and services are sold at a high price, but that they often offer a counterfeit form of grace. True grace, as Spurgeon and others have pointed out, isn’t about what you can buy or how you can look. It’s not about how much you can give or what you can wear—it’s about what God has already freely given to you. And that, no matter how hard empires try to sell it, cannot be commodified.

### Conclusion: The True Price of Glory

As we explore the grift of glory, it becomes clear that the commercialization of religion and faith is more than just an economic issue. It’s a profound moral and spiritual question. The gospel, as we see in Spurgeon’s work, is about sacrificial love, not profit. The stories of exploitation that Grann uncovers in *Killers of the Flower Moon* remind us of the price of commodifying humanity. The grift of glory is not just about financial gain—it’s about stealing something sacred from the human experience and turning it into a product to be sold.

In the end, we must ask ourselves: when we buy into these commodified versions of faith, what is the cost? Are we willing to exchange the gospel of grace for a shiny, branded version that promises everything but delivers nothing of true value?
